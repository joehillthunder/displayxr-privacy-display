How it works on DisplayXR

Samsung's Privacy Display (Galaxy S26 Ultra) steers each pixel's light with Flex Magic Pixel hardware, so people beside you see a dark, unreadable screen. A DisplayXR panel has something different to work with: it tracks the viewer's eyes and steers a separate image to each eye. This demo builds privacy from that.

  • Presence. When the tracker has nobody (trackingState is searching), private content hides within 80 ms.
  • Privacy cone. The tracked eyes give the viewer's angle and distance. Outside ±30° (±15° with maximum protection), private content hides. This is the software twin of a narrowed viewing angle.
  • Owner lock. The first steady viewer is enrolled by their interpupillary distance (IPD), a 3D measurement that does not change when you turn your head. If the eyes jump in one frame (the tracker switched to another face), tracking drops and comes back, or the IPD stops matching, content hides until the owner is verified again.
  • Stereo-only secrets. Private text becomes a random-dot stereogram. Each eye's image is uniform noise; the text exists only in the difference between the two images, so it appears, floating in depth, only for the tracked pair of eyes. A bystander catching one view (or a mix of both), a photo, or a screen recording of one eye sees noise. The dots re-seed every few frames, so a long exposure averages to grey.

Limits. The runtime tracks one face and does not report a second one, so a shoulder-surfer is not detected directly. Viewer locks protect when the owner is absent, off-axis or replaced. Stereo-only secrets protect while the owner is looking, but a lenticular panel repeats its views, so someone standing exactly in a repeat zone could still catch a usable pair. A camera with two lenses can also recover the text.
